If you've read anything about food safety in India recently, you've
probably seen the name Sunset Yellow FCF. It's one of the most talked
about synthetic colours in the country, and one of the first things people
ask about when a tea or beverage brand comes under scrutiny. Here's what
it actually is.

## The basics

Sunset Yellow FCF (INS 110) is a synthetic, petroleum-derived dye that
produces a bright orange-yellow colour. It's cheap, stable, and doesn't
fade the way natural colourants can which is exactly why it's used so
widely in processed food and drink around the world, from soft drinks to
sauces to confectionery.

It is not a natural ingredient in tea, coffee, or any plant product. If
it's present in a tea, it was added.

## Where it's actually allowed

Sunset Yellow FCF is one of a small set of synthetic colours India's food
regulator permits but only in specific food categories, and only up to
a maximum level, typically capped in the range of 100–200 mg/kg depending
on the category. Permitted does not mean permitted everywhere.

Tea is not one of the categories where added synthetic colour is
permitted. That's not our interpretation it's written directly into how
laboratories report the test. When a tea sample is tested for Sunset
Yellow FCF, the specification isn't "must be under some limit." It's
"should be absent." Anything else is a fail, at any concentration.

## Why it gets tested for specifically

Sunset Yellow FCF and a handful of other dyes Tartrazine, Ponceau 4R,
Carmoisine, Erythrosine, Indigo Carmine, Brilliant Blue FCF, Fast Green
FCF form the standard panel labs run when checking a food product for
undeclared synthetic colour. It's not that Sunset Yellow FCF is uniquely
dangerous among the eight; it's that it's cheap, produces a colour close
to the reddish-brown of brewed tea, and has historically been the one most
often found where it shouldn't be.
