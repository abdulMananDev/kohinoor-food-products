Instant tea premix the kind that turns into a cup of tea with hot water
and a spoon gets asked about a lot, usually right after someone reads a
scary headline about a specific brand. The honest answer is: it depends
entirely on what's in the specific pack in your hand, not on the category
as a whole. Here's what to actually look for.

## What's usually in it

A typical instant tea mix premix is built from a handful of ingredients:

- **Tea solids or tea powder** the actual tea component
- **Milk solids** for the "milk tea" taste without needing fresh milk
- **Sugar or sweetener**
- **Creamer** (often non-dairy)
- **Flavouring**, and sometimes **added colour**, to standardise the look
  of the finished cup from batch to batch

None of that is inherently unsafe. Instant coffee and drinking chocolate
premixes work on the same basic idea. The question that matters is
whether every one of those ingredients is on the permitted list, in
permitted quantities, and declared on the label and whether the specific
batch has actually been checked.

## The part that causes problems

Added colour is the ingredient most likely to cause trouble, for one
simple reason: it's the easiest thing to add cheaply to make a weak or
inconsistent brew look richer and more uniform. Synthetic dyes like Sunset
Yellow FCF or Tartrazine can do that at a fraction of the cost of getting
the underlying tea quality right.

But as we've written about separately, [regulators don't permit added
synthetic colour in tea at all](/blog/what-is-sunset-yellow-fcf) the
specification a lab tests against is "should be absent," not "under a
limit." So its presence in a tea premix isn't a grey area; it's either
there or it isn't.

## What you can actually check

You mostly can't tell from taste or colour alone whether something has
been added that's the whole problem colour additives solve for a
manufacturer. What you can check:

1. **The ingredients list** anything added should be declared. If a
   product doesn't list its full composition, that's worth noticing.
2. **Whether the brand has published lab results**, and whether those
   results are dated, tied to a specific batch, and from an
   NABL-accredited laboratory rather than an in-house claim.
3. **Whether "not tested" and "tested and passed" are being distinguished
   clearly.** A brand that never mentions testing at all is not the same
   as a brand publishing negative results, and neither is automatically
   worse than a brand with a slick "100% pure" label and no report behind
   it.
