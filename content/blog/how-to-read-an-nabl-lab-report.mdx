When a brand says "lab tested," the report itself is the only part worth
trusting — not the sentence summarising it. Here's how to actually read
one, using the kind of report a food testing lab produces, field by field.

## Start with the accreditation, not the result

Before looking at any number, check who did the testing. **NABL** — the
National Accreditation Board for Testing and Calibration Laboratories — is
the body that accredits labs in India against the international standard
ISO/IEC 17025. Accreditation means the lab's methods, equipment, and
competence have been independently audited; it isn't a claim the lab
makes about itself.

Every accredited lab has a **certificate number** (formatted like
TC-XXXXX), and an **issue date** and **valid until date**. Two things are
worth checking:

1. Was the certificate valid on the date the report was issued? A lab
   whose accreditation lapsed before the test doesn't carry the same
   weight.
2. Does the lab's accredited **scope** actually cover what it tested? NABL
   certificates list the specific fields a lab is accredited for — you can
   look this up independently at nabl-india.org rather than taking a
   brand's word for it.

## The report header

Most test reports carry the same block of identifying information near
the top:

- **Report number** — a unique reference for that specific report. Worth
  noting down if you ever need to query it with the lab.
- **Name and address of customer** — who submitted the sample. This isn't
  always the brand itself; sometimes it's a distributor or retailer, which
  is worth noticing either way.
- **Sample description and code** — what was tested, including any batch
  number marked on the sample.
- **Date of receipt, date analysis started, date analysis completed** —
  the actual timeline of testing, separate from when the report was
  written up.

If a report is missing dates, a report number, or a clear description of
what was sampled, treat it with more scepticism, not less.

## The results table

This is usually laid out as: **parameter tested**, **unit** (commonly
mg/kg), **result obtained**, **test method used**, and **specification**
(the limit or requirement it's being checked against).

A few things matter here that are easy to skim past:

- **"Not Detected" is not the same as "zero."** It means the substance
  wasn't found above the method's detection threshold — the smallest
  amount that method can reliably pick up. It's an honest limitation of
  analytical chemistry, not a loophole.
- **The specification tells you what "pass" actually means.** For some
  parameters it's a numeric ceiling (say, under 100 mg/kg). For others —
  synthetic colours in tea, for instance — it may simply read "should be
  absent," meaning any detectable amount is a fail.
- **The test method matters.** HPLC (High-Performance Liquid
  Chromatography) is a standard, reliable method for detecting synthetic
  dyes at low concentrations. If a report doesn't state its method, you
  can't independently judge how sensitive the test actually was.

## What the report doesn't tell you

Two limits are easy to miss:

- **A report only covers what was tested.** A clean result on seven
  synthetic colours says nothing about an eighth colour that wasn't part
  of the panel, or about pesticide residue, heavy metals, or
  microbiological quality if those weren't tested.
- **A report only covers the sample tested.** One batch's report doesn't
  extend to a different batch, even of the same product.

Any lab note at the bottom of the report about the result applying "only
to the tested sample and applicable parameters" is worth reading as
carefully as the results table itself — it's the lab drawing the boundary
of its own claim.
